Quality · testing · delivery

How much quality is enough?

These are my views on software quality and testing, organized around how software ships now: AI writing and checking code, pipelines that deploy many times a day, and fixes that reach production in hours. The definition of quality hasn't changed. What counts as "enough" has, and it depends on how fast you can find a problem and undo it.

01

What quality is

Software quality can mean many things. Any definition will vary with the context, and with the perspective from which it is seen.

Gerald Weinberg suggested "Quality is value to some person." James Bach and Michael Bolton extended this to "Quality is value to some person who matters." A more recent definition by David Greenlees states "Quality is value to some person who matters, at some point in time." That last clause matters more now than when it was written. The point in time keeps moving: what was good enough at 9 a.m. can be fixed by noon.

These lead to competing ideas of quality, which may or may not be valid. Customers, partners and developers may have different definitions of what good, quality software is.

A customer or partner might say

  • My data and transactions are secure.
  • The service or feature is available.
  • The service or feature works as it should.
  • The service or feature is usable in a timely manner.

A developer might say

  • I can easily understand the code; it's maintainable and extendable.
  • I can continuously test the code, and the defects found keep dropping in severity and number.
  • The code is secure, and the design meets all non-functional benchmarks.

The right definition is important in an organization, so that everyone in the stakeholder chain has a shared understanding, measures themselves against common standards, and improves toward a common goal. Here is one I wrote and used at one company, at one point in time:

"Quality is making sure we give a consistent customer and partner experience using our services, which will help customer retention and future partnerships."

That definition is not universal. It fit that company's business, its customers and partners, and that moment. That's Greenlees's point applied to my own words: the person who matters and the point in time are part of the definition. Your organization needs its own, written for its context, and revisited when the context changes. What carries over is the shape: name who the quality is for, and what they need to keep getting.

To satisfy that statement we needed software that is reliable, performant, secure, usable and functional, to name a few. The way the product is designed, the way code is written, the way it is verified and the platform it is deployed on all play a part in how the product is perceived quality-wise.

Principles under the definition

  • Quality of software is a mindset, and NOT the responsibility of a few members of the team alone.
  • "Testing is an empirical technical investigation done to provide stakeholders with information about the quality of the product or service under test." (Kaner)
  • Testing is one of the factors contributing to quality, but there are others.
  • Testing is a skilled development activity, like writing code and like reviews; it starts when we start planning the work.
  • Testing shows the presence of mistakes; it can only reduce the number of unfound issues.
  • Exhaustive testing is impossible.
  • The cost of an error grows as it moves through the delivery lifecycle, so testing must begin at the earliest stages. (Section 02 says where that curve has flattened, and where it hasn't.)

Two ways to get there

Defect management counts and manages defects, usually by severity. A defect is any failure to address end-user requirements: missed or misunderstood requirements, errors in design, logic, data relationships, timing, validity checks, and code. This is generally a reactive approach.

Software quality attributes assesses conformance with a quality model such as ISO/IEC 25010 (section 14), and guides testing of both functional and non-functional attributes. This is generally a proactive approach. A good team does both.

How much is enough updated

If we can put a fix out in a few hours, how much quality do we need before we ship, and how much testing?

The classic answer is the cost curve. A defect removed after the product has gone into production costs around 100 times more than one removed during requirements (Magowan). "When it comes to software quality, you can pay now or pay more later" (Wiegers). Too often, to move fast, teams skip some or all of the recommended tests, and "our roadmap this quarter is full" is the reason given.

That math is still right for some changes. But it was measured when shipping meant a quarterly release, a change board, and weeks to get a fix out. When a fix ships in hours and a flag turns a feature off in seconds, "later" got a lot cheaper, for some defects. So the useful question isn't "how much testing?" It's "what does it cost if this is wrong, and for how long?"

The doors that only open one way

Some changes don't get cheaper no matter how fast you deploy. For these the old bar stands: test before, as if you can't roll back, because you can't.

  • Money. A charge taken twice, a refund not issued, a transfer sent to the wrong place.
  • Data. Records written wrong, migrations, deletes, backfills. Rolling back code doesn't roll back rows.
  • Security and PII. You can't un-leak data.
  • Legal and regulatory. Accessibility law, privacy rules. Failure here is failure of the product, no matter how defect-free it has been working.
  • Messages. Emails, texts and push notifications can't be recalled.
  • Trust. A customer who hit the same bug three times doesn't come back when you fix it.

The Definition of Done section quotes a line I've always liked: done will be defined very differently if you're building firmware for pacemakers or an e-commerce portal. Inside one product, the checkout and the marketing banner are the pacemaker and the portal.

The precondition

"We can just fix it forward" is a claim, and it needs evidence. If you've never measured how long it takes you to notice a problem and roll it back, you don't know that it's fast. Measure MTTD and MTTR (rule two), rehearse the rollback, and only then lower the pre-merge bar. Until then, fast iteration is a hope, not a control.

Two rules I don't bend

Everything else on this page is context and judgment. These two are not.

Доверяй, но проверяй

doveryai, no proveryai · trust, but verify

I test to see, not to confirm. A green check is a claim. A passing build is a claim. An AI agent saying "done, all tests pass" is a claim. I trust the people and the tools, and I still look at the output.

Verify the thing that matters: what is served, not what is committed; what the test asserted, not that it was green; the element the healed locator found, not that it found one.

If you didn't measure it, it didn't happen

no measurement · no proof · no learning

A management cliché says what gets measured gets done. And what does not get measured does not get done, because it is easy to ignore. A deploy without a metric, a fix without a before-and-after, a "faster" with no timing: none of it happened.

Measure outcomes, not activity. Read numbers to learn, never as a target: the moment a metric becomes a goal, it gets gamed.

The two work together. Measurement is how you verify at scale; verification is how you know the measurement isn't lying. Section 11 covers what to measure; section 08 covers what verification looks like when the code, the tests and the reviewer may all be AI.

What testing is for

Testing has nine objectives. At high delivery speed, number seven does most of the work.

  1. Prevent defects. Avoid mistakes in the early stages. Prevention means root cause analysis of past defects, then specific measures so those types don't happen again.
  2. Evaluate work products such as requirements, design, user stories and code. Find ambiguity or contradictions before a developer picks the work up. Reviews, walk-throughs and static analysis happen before code integrates. This is verification.
  3. Verify that all specified requirements are met. Know which tests cover which requirements, so gaps are visible. The old factory-QA version of this was a ponderous traceability matrix document. I want the lightweight version: tag tests with the story or acceptance criterion they cover, and let the pipeline report what has no test. AI can help find the gaps and draft the mapping, but the output stays small. I'm here to test and deliver quality, not to raise my page count.
  4. Validate that the product works as users and stakeholders expect. This is validation: checking the product after development, manually or automated, opaque box or transparent box, and by stakeholders through acceptance testing.
  5. Build confidence in the quality level. Fewer defects, higher satisfaction, lower maintenance costs.
  6. Reduce the risk of insufficient quality. Every project carries uncertainty: launch time, budget, technology, implementation, maintenance. Testing brings risk management in early so risks are found as soon as possible.
  7. Give stakeholders enough information to make informed decisions, especially about the level of quality. Be transparent: coverage, what is missing, what went wrong, and the constraints and risks behind them.
  8. Find defects, as early as possible. Unit testing finds them where they're cheapest; integration testing finds them at the touchpoints between systems; regression testing makes sure changes didn't add new ones; operational testing checks reliability and performance under maximum load.
  9. Comply with contractual, legal and regulatory requirements, and verify compliance. Accessibility law differs by country; the EU has strict rules on handling personal data. Failure here will fail the product no matter how defect-free it has been working. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 covers the testing standards themselves.

Why seven? When you ship many times a day, nobody reads a test report before each deploy. The pipeline, the dashboards and the alerts are the report, and the decision to ship is made by a gate or by a person looking at a canary graph. Testing's job is to make sure that information is there, correct, and in front of whoever decides.

The test layers

Where testing should live, and how much at each layer. Current thinking is more of a trophy than a pyramid.

Many of you have seen versions of the pyramid where layers got bigger or smaller depending on the thinking or bias of the presenter. Automated testing should be the primary approach at every layer, especially for repeatable, stable scenarios. Manual testing is for targeted exploratory work, usability reviews, and complex or high-risk features, particularly new functionality where automation doesn't yet give enough coverage.

What every layer has in common

  • Tests run on every code change and every pipeline run, including on the developer's machine before check-in.
  • Failure to pass 100% of tests fails the pipeline or blocks the commit until fixed. A flaky test is fixed or quarantined, not retried until green.
  • Results are captured in a standard format (JUnit/xUnit or similar) and pushed to a data store, so pass/fail rates can be gathered over time. See rule two.
StaticChecked without running the codeReviews of stories, designs and code; static analysis; security scanning.+

Static testing checks the software without executing it, two ways: review (requirements, design documents, source, test plans and cases, help and page content) and static analysis, where tools analyze code for structural defects.

Finds: undefined values, inconsistent interfaces between modules, unused variables, dead code, standards violations, security vulnerabilities, syntax violations, and requirements errors from misunderstanding.

Approach: story reviews before implementation; peer review of every change; IDEs and tools configured for conventions; static analysis and a security scan in the pipeline.

AI-assisted: AI code review that flags security issues and standards violations; requirements analysis that finds ambiguity, contradictions and gaps before coding; documentation review for outdated or inconsistent docs.

When: every commit and pipeline run. Failures block the commit or deployment.

UnitA section of code, in isolationFunctions, methods, modules, objects. Where coverage is easy to measure.+

Finds: local data structure issues, boundary conditions, error-handling paths, variable issues, incorrect initialization, wrong expressions and arithmetic precedence.

Value: finds bugs early, when they're fastest to fix; testable code is usually well-decoupled and more maintainable; tests document the author's intent; refactoring becomes safe; it shows the implementation solved the problem.

Approach: TDD. Add a test for each defect found. Run in CI. Watch coverage as a trend, not a target (section 11).

AI-assisted: generate unit tests from code changes; find untested paths; flag flaky tests from run history. Generated tests should be checked against the requirement, not just the code: a test written from the code asserts what the code does, not what it should do.

Teams with low unit coverage find more defects at higher layers, where they take far more effort to find and fix.

IntegrationUnits and components, combinedIncluding contract tests, component accessibility and visual regression.+

After unit testing, units are combined and tested for function, performance and reliability between them. You may need test doubles for complex data. If you stub or mock external APIs, also run contract tests against the real endpoints to verify the contract.

Finds: interface mismatches, bad sequencing, communication failures between components, incorrect assumptions.

Value: fast (no multiple systems to talk to); easy to maintain and debug (the problem is in the component you're testing, often down to the line or endpoint); repeatable, with no flaky external dependencies; scales, because components test independently; runs on developer machines before code is pushed.

Approach: write tests, not too many, mostly integration. Mock API responses. Test from the consumer's point of view. TDD, again. Manual contract reviews and user-perspective walk-throughs of integration points.

Examples: Testing Library, Mock Service Worker, Pact for consumer-driven contracts, supertest, WireMock, Karate.

AI-assisted: analyze contracts and usage to suggest tests and catch breaking changes; synthesize realistic, varied test data; flag unusual patterns in results and logs.

SystemThe application being built, end to endIts own workflows, UI or API, with test doubles at its boundaries.+

System testing runs the workflow of the application being built from beginning to end, replicating real user scenarios. Keep it to a minimal set of critical business flows and push the rest down the stack. If architecture forces a lot of testing here, invest in stubbing external dependencies rather than relying on real data and long setup.

Finds: issues with other services, databases and applications; UI consistency; device or browser issues; incorrect assumptions; and, depending on lower-layer coverage, defects that should have been caught lower.

Approach:

  • Only automate critical business functionality here: the $$$ flows and the high-risk areas.
  • Document tests in BDD form; ideally the stories already are.
  • Automate golden paths; leave error and edge paths to integration and unit layers.
  • Test across devices and browsers; your top five by traffic is probably sufficient.
  • Optimize setup and teardown; create test data automatically and clean up after yourself.
  • Keep tests deterministic: fix or quarantine non-deterministic ones.
  • Keep tests discrete: each stands on its own, with setup, test and teardown inside it.
  • Add time-boxed exploratory sessions for each new feature.

Examples: Playwright, axe for accessibility, Lighthouse, Karate.

AI-assisted: visual regression; test healing when locators or APIs change; risk-based suggestions for exploratory testing; accessibility scanning; user-journey analysis from production data; test generation from stories; flaky-test management; clustering failures for root cause.

Use test doubles in PR branches and real dependencies after merge, as a limited subset to avoid data issues.

End to endThe whole business flow, across appsReal services, real data, no test doubles. Few of these.+

E2E tests cross multiple applications and services, which makes them harder to keep stable when the underlying services change. That often means false positives and flakiness. Used as the main gate in an application's pipeline, they bring flakiness, failures and long test cycles. Same tools as system tests, wider scope, and the same rule: a minimal set of critical business flows.

System vs end to end, with an example. Say the app under test is a post-purchase page that needs a logged-in user with a completed order.

  • System test: simulate login with a cookie that the login app would normally set, and pass the order id as a query parameter to the page where the test starts. You skip the flows and applications you aren't testing. For APIs, string a few calls together to simulate the workflow.
  • E2E test: automate every step a user takes: sign in, shop, check out, pay, and finally reach the post-purchase page. For APIs, the workflow crosses multiple services.

CI / CD / CT / CO

Continuous integration, delivery, testing and operations, across the six phases a change goes through.

Build time and run time

The pipeline splits in two at the deployed artifact: phases 1–3 test code, phases 4–6 test something running.

Build-time tests run during compilation, with no deployed artifact, so a developer can test the project's own logic quickly and in isolation. For UI apps: static, unit, integration (component). For services: static and unit.

Run-time tests run against deployed artifacts. For UI apps: system, and end to end if required. For services: integration and system.

The application under test (system under test) is the single application in development. Downstream services aren't part of it unless their code is in the same repository. Tests may exercise the dependencies, but they don't target them.

The phases

Pre-coding. The idea is created and approved; stories, documentation and reviews happen before coding starts. Asking questions early is also an act of testing. Bring the quality perspective into every engineering decision, alongside product and development: it helps everyone see the scope and the alternate and negative flows, which are where bugs live.

Pre-commit. Development on the local machine before pushing: pairing, local review, and every test that can run locally.

Pre-merge to main. Code is pushed, CI runs, and a deployed artifact lands in the test environment.

Post-merge to main. Reviewed and merged; the post-merge CI steps lead to a production-deployable artifact.

Production deploy. The artifact is deployed to production.

Production go-live. The artifact is live and taking traffic, or the feature flag is enabled.

Continuous integration

"Continuous Integration does not get rid of bugs, but it does make them dramatically easier to find and remove."

Martin Fowler

Commit daily, if not every few hours. The point is that nothing new breaks existing functionality, and that testing doesn't pile up at the end. When a test fails, we know which change caused it, and that person can fix it quickly. Keep tasks and branches as small as possible; it makes the code easier to write and the review easier to do. Before a review, the new code should have tests and they should pass. Code review is not the primary bug-finding instrument; a robust set of unit, functional and integration tests is.

Continuous delivery

CI works only when continuous delivery and continuous testing are practiced too. We are continuously delivering software to somebody, somewhere: to production or to lower environments. The best organizations release many times a day. Small, frequent releases deliver value without delays and keep each change small enough to understand and undo. Releases are also a communication problem, not only a technical one: tell people what shipped and what's still open.

Continuous testing

Whenever a change is made, big or small, make sure it doesn't break existing code or functionality. Unit and API tests cover the code impact; a regression subset covers the major flows. Releasing without regression is heavily risky. Cloud browser and device platforms make the compatibility matrix practical. Continuous testing does not stop at pre-production (section 07).

Continuous operations

The CO is what makes the other three safe to do fast: running what's live and watching it, all the time. Deploy, release, monitor, roll back, learn, repeat. Observability is the part that sees. It's a property of the system; monitoring is actively watching it. Logs, metrics, traces and alerts are the pillars. Section 07 covers why this is testing too.

Shift left and shift right

Shift left: testing starts in the envisioning phase. Test planning and strategy design start before code. Teams drive the discussion on what testing is needed, identify acceptance criteria, and outline high-level test cases. Test activities are part of story estimation.

Shift right: testing continues in production, where complexity exists in its highest form. Dogfooding, synthetics, canaries, chaos experiments and incident learning. Both directions, at once.

After the deploy updated

Sufficiently advanced monitoring is indistinguishable from testing.

with apologies to Arthur C. Clarke

A test is a known input, an expected result, a check, and a report. A synthetic check that logs in every minute and places a test order is that same thing, run against production instead of a build. An SLO alert is an assertion: "p99 checkout latency under 800 ms" is an expected result. Real user monitoring is exploratory testing done by your customers, at a scale no team can match. You just have to be able to see what they found.

The difference is who pays when it fails. A failing test in CI costs a red build. A failing check in production costs some users a bad experience. Monitoring is testing where the test data is real people. That's an acceptable trade when reach is small and recovery is fast (section 02). It is not acceptable for the one-way doors.

What makes monitoring count as testing

  • It has an oracle. SLIs with SLOs, not dashboards someone might look at. If nothing asserts, nothing is tested.
  • It's fast. Time to detect in minutes, not when a customer calls support.
  • It's tied to the change. You can tell which deploy or which flag caused the movement.
  • It acts. An SLO breach during a canary rolls back without waiting for a human.
  • It runs without traffic. Synthetic checks on critical flows run at 3 a.m. too, when no real user would find the break.
  • It's part of Done. Monitors and alerts are added or adapted with the story, not after the incident.

Three questions every team should be able to answer: How do we know our app is working right now? What monitors do we have in place? Which critical flows have synthetics?

The release toolbox

Feature flags and dark launching. Deploy the code with the feature off. Turn it on for the team, then internal users, then a percentage of customers. Deploy and release become two decisions, and "release" can be undone in seconds without a deploy.

Canary releases. Run the new version in production with a slice of real traffic and a safe rollback strategy. It tests capacity, and captures how the new version affects production before everyone gets it.

A/B testing. Diligent use of A/B tests and flags gives the team flexibility, and gauges feature readiness from the customer's side: how it behaves in the real world, not only whether it works.

Rollback. One action, rehearsed, measured. If rollback is a runbook nobody has run, it isn't a rollback. Database changes use expand/contract so the previous version still works against the new schema; otherwise code rollback is a fiction.

Testing in production and dogfooding. The approach differs from lower environments. Testing in production is more of a manual exercise, with homework done first on what to test and what not to. It finds problems before customers do, and builds empathy with them.

Controlled chaos experiments. Chaos engineering finds weaknesses conventional QA can't, from known knowns to unknown unknowns:

  1. Define the current steady state with a baseline. Start small, such as network utilization.
  2. Define optimal conditions: a normal day, an easy day, and a very hard day. The last is the stress test.
  3. Form a hypothesis about where and how the system will break.
  4. Run a real-world event, for example taking down a firewall that cuts connectivity to an ISP.
  5. Validate the hypothesis. Watch utilization and throughput, see where it fell over, and look for anything unexpected. Stabilize, document, remediate.

Incident learning. Despite our best efforts, incidents and outages happen. Analyze them, do a real root cause analysis, and fold the learning (functional, performance, security, whatever it was) back into the team's practices and the product. Track the corrective actions. The incident becomes a test, a monitor, or a line in the Definition of Done.

Observability. Nothing beats an environment that can tell you its own health. Collect the right logs, track metrics, detect anything that could affect the user experience, and alert on what matters most. When something goes wrong, tracing shows how a user got there: the functions, the parameters, how deep they got, and their whole journey through the stack. Dashboards built from that data show errors, usage and the success rate of new functionality.

AI in the loop updated

AI is augmenting both manual and automated testing. It speeds up test creation, improves coverage, detects anomalies, and helps with exploratory work.

Manual testing: people run test cases or explore the application to find what scripts miss. Automated testing: tools and scripts check functionality, usually in the pipeline. AI-assisted testing: machine learning, language models and agents that generate tests, prioritize them, analyze results, and find patterns humans or scripts might miss.

Where it pays off

In the pipeline

  • Test impact analysis: run the tests this change can affect, first.
  • Test healing when UI locators or APIs change.
  • Flaky test detection and quarantine.
  • Prioritizing critical and failure-prone tests.

In triage

  • Anomaly detection in logs and results.
  • Clustering similar failures so one root cause is one ticket.
  • Root cause suggestions from logs, traces and the diff.

In data and setup

  • Realistic, varied synthetic test data.
  • Robust locator selection for UI automation.
  • Test cases from requirements, stories or recorded flows.

Everywhere else

  • Visual regression, accessibility scanning, security findings.
  • Suggesting high-risk or untested areas to explore.
  • Requirements-to-test traceability and gap analysis.
  • Finding obsolete or redundant tests.
  • Performance regression detection.

What changes when AI writes the code

Code got cheap. Being wrong didn't. AI makes writing code fast, so there is more change, more often. Verification becomes the bottleneck, and the tester's job gets bigger, not smaller.

The output is a claim. Rule one applies to agents the same as to people. "Done, all tests pass" gets checked: which tests, what they asserted, what is actually served.

Don't let the same model grade its own homework. Tests generated from the code assert what the code does. Tests should come from the requirement or the acceptance criteria, which is one more reason to write those well. BDD-style acceptance criteria turn out to be excellent prompts.

Healing needs a gate. A healed test that passes against the wrong element is worse than a failing test, because it hides the failure. Heal the locator, then prove the new one finds the same element, and keep a person in the loop for anything that changes what is asserted. In my own healing work the number I watch is "wrong heals kept," and it has to stay at zero. I built this into my open-source Python and TypeScript Playwright frameworks.

Measure the AI. What did it catch, what did it miss, how often was it wrong, and what did it cost? If you can't answer that, you don't know whether it helps (rule two).

The warning that hasn't changed

Relying only on AI-assisted approaches or automation creates a false sense of security. These tools may not catch issues that only emerge through real user interaction or unexpected workflows. Combine manual, automated and AI-assisted testing deliberately, and the team gets broader coverage, finds more subtle issues, and delivers a better product.

People still test

Automation can't judge whether a flow makes sense. It can't test usability. Somebody has to write each automated flow and tell the machine what to do. So what's the deal with manual testing?

Back in 2000, automated tools pretty much sucked and cost $20k a seat or more; manual testing was "the way." In 2008 manual was still the main way, with a small set of automated tests augmenting. Around 2010 the real shift toward automation began, and by 2015 automated testing was heavily used, understood and trusted. Now there are very few manual testers around, and a lot of that skill set is gone. Teams need to deliver fast and automation is key to that, but there is still room for manual practice: exploratory work on edge cases, boundaries, error handling and usability. I'm not saying a team needs a dedicated person doing this full time. The team itself needs to be capable of effective manual testing in those areas, and product should look at the app from the customer's view too.

Here's how that works in practice.

Developer 1

Developer 1 grabs a ticket. They code furiously to do the best they can. They use TDD to write unit tests, they create or expand the integration tests, and heck, they even write a system test that exercises the new functionality. AWESOME. Along the way they open the app to check navigation for that system test. Guess what: that's manual testing. Then, to be super thorough, they open the app and try some field validation: too many characters, numbers in a text field, navigating away and back. There are a lot of heuristics for testing new code. All tests pass, so they open a PR.

The PR build runs the new test and it fails. What the...? Locally they were using stubbed data; now it's real (test) data, and there's a mismatch. More manual effort: digging through logs, using the browser dev tools. Found it. Fix the code, rerun the tests, update the stubs, re-test the area by hand, paying attention to similar spots. Push. Green across the board.

Developer 2

During review, Developer 2 notices something that might be a problem, opens the app in test, and tries what they think will break. Bam. The new code has the same issue seen nine months ago in a similar function that needed hardening against certain data. More manual testing. They point Developer 1 at the old fix; the change is made, tests updated, a new stub and test added to prove it, more manual validation. Push another revision.

Product

At demo, product sees what was built, and something seems off. It works, but the new field's placement makes sense inside the component and not on the page; users could miss it until they submit and get an error. Product clicks around, pins down the usability issue, requests a change, then reopens the app and confirms the flow is better. All checks pass, merge to main. Feature in production about 45 minutes later.

Developer 1 did manual verification plus boundary and heuristic exploration, missed a defect, and fixed it quickly. Developer 2 reviewed the code and manually checked the area they expected trouble in, and found a real issue. Product sensed a problem they couldn't name and used manual exploration to find a usability issue. This is the new world of manual testing: techniques and heuristics for getting the result you need in the limited time you have.

Rewrite that story today and an AI agent is in it: drafting the tests, reviewing the PR, maybe flagging the nine-month-old pattern. It doesn't replace any of the three people. It gives each of them more to check.

Exploratory testing and dojos

As features grow, you test more, and every fixed bug needs to stay fixed. Automation makes that possible. Manual testing is still worth doing, focused on exploration, where the goal is to uncover non-obvious errors. Let computers do what they're good at, repetition, and reserve people for thinking and the most imaginative scenarios.

  • Time-box a session to at most two hours, with a clear scope and a short, simple charter.
  • Run a dojo for significant new capability, with all impacted teams and stakeholders.
  • Do it after development testing is finished, when the feature needs an objective look.
  • Aim at the unknown knowns (issues you aren't aware of but would understand) and the unknown unknowns.
  • Buddy or pair testing: a developer has a buddy test the code before delivering it, for direct, immediate feedback.

AI can draft charters and point at risky areas. People decide what's worth an afternoon.

Definition of Done

Before we cut a single line of code, we need to decide what done means for our product.

It will be defined very differently if you're building firmware for pacemakers or an e-commerce portal. Without a Definition of Done we don't know what working software means, and without working software we can't have predictable delivery.

Characteristics

  • A short, measurable checklist. Things whose outcome can be tested, preferably automatically.
  • Mirrors shippable. At review, the product owner should be able to say "That's awesome, let's ship it."
  • No further work. Anything more needed to ship means you weren't done, and it eats next iteration's capacity. Ideally delivery is fully automated.

Three components

Business or functional requirements: the user stories and their acceptance criteria. Non-functional requirements: performance, accessibility, security, usability, localization, availability, compliance. Putting them in the DoD bakes them in at the story level. Quality standards: how we build the product right.

Examples to put on a DoD

  • Static analysis passes with no critical errors. Starting from legacy? Cap the count ("no more than 50") and lower it over time.
  • Code coverage stays the same or goes up. A fixed coverage number like 90% is a red herring and tells you nothing about code quality; an adverse change in coverage tells you something.
  • Agreed engineering standards met, automated where possible.
  • Acceptance criteria pass, and acceptance tests are automated. If you think something will break, have a test for it.
  • Security checks pass. You won't find every issue, but don't ship the ones we already know about.
  • Agreed UX, accessibility and architecture guidelines met.
  • PR reviewed by at least one other person, including AI-generated code.
  • No known bug caused by the story.
  • Validated in pre-production.
  • Monitoring and alerts added or adapted as part of the story.
  • New: risky changes ship behind a flag; the rollback path is known and has been exercised; a synthetic check covers any new critical flow.

Growing it

The DoD doesn't magically appear, and the software doesn't magically comply. Seed it in a workshop with the whole team and the expertise you need (code, test, security, UX, architecture), then grow it. Reflect on it every retrospective, and add to it mid-sprint if something is missing. If a problem means you don't have working software, stop and fix it before adding features, then raise the DoD so future increments meet it. If it's smaller, put the work in the backlog, resolve it, and pin the outcome by adding it to the DoD. The next increment can only reach the quality bar of all those before it.

Measuring it

A limited set of useful metrics, defined for balance, automated, and interpreted the same way by everyone.

Define metrics from specific objectives for the project, process or product. Define them for balance, because a single metric can give a misleading picture. Agree on their interpretation with all stakeholders before you discuss the numbers. Resist defining too many. Automate collection and reporting so measurement doesn't eat the time it's meant to save. And watch for variation that reflects something other than what you agreed it meant.

Delivery: the DORA metrics

DORA is the widely adopted standard for delivery performance, and several of its measures are direct measures of automation and quality.

MetricWhat it isWhat it says about quality
Deployment frequencyHow often you successfully release to productionNeeds a robust, reliable pipeline and tests
Lead time for changesCommit to productionSlow tests and manual gates show up here
Change failure rate% of deployments causing a failure in productionA direct measure of how effective the testing is
Time to restoreFailure to recoveryThe "time" lever from section 02

Quality and testing

Product risks: % of risks fully covered by passing tests; % with some or all tests failing; % not yet fully tested; coverage by risk category; risks found after the initial analysis.

Defects: found vs. fixed over time; mean time between failures; breakdown by root cause, phase introduced/detected/removed, priority, rejected or duplicate, leaked to production, and defect age.

Coverage: requirements and design, risk, environment and configuration, and code.

Operations: MTTD (average time to detect) and MTTR (detection plus recovery), rollbacks, build success rate, test success rate, flaky rate.

Confidence: often reported subjectively. Measure it through surveys, or use coverage as a surrogate, and say which you did.

Business: map engineering metrics to business ones. A shorter time to restore means better customer satisfaction. Change failure rate, defect escape rate, usage, churn, conversion after a release, repeat purchase.

AI: catches, misses, false positives, wrong heals, and cost per useful finding.

A scorecard, not a target

A quality scorecard measures software against KPIs and your quality policy, and shows pipeline strengths and weaknesses: commits, production deploys, builds, lead time, CI/CD adoption, change failure rate, rollbacks, MTTD, MTTR, build and test success rates. A balanced scorecard connects the dots between the work, the measures, and the goals. Read it to learn. The moment one number becomes the goal, it gets gamed, and you're measuring the gaming.

Defects, risk, debt

Defects are inevitable. How many escape to production, and how long they stay, can be controlled.

Defect management

Detect, triage, resolve, analyze. Capture the right data with each defect so the person fixing it, possibly on another team, can make an informed decision. Triage often; an unprioritized defect is a risk lying unattended. Give each priority an SLA you can actually meet, and track the ones past it.

PriorityDefinitionFix SLA
P1 BlockerService unavailable, memory leaks, unreliable data, performance degradationImmediate / 1 dayMitigate in minutes: roll back or flag off. The fix can follow.
P2 CriticalCritical functionality broken, no workaround14 days
P3 NormalNot fully working, workaround available30 days
P4 LowCosmetic issues, typos60 days
P5 TrivialDoesn't affect functionality90 days

SLAs can change at the team's discretion. Then analyze the data: defect types, resolution trends, backlog, unprioritized defects. Share the lessons. Have a prevention culture that drives defects toward zero, and feed what you learn back into review, development and test practice.

Risk

Risk is the possibility of a negative or undesirable outcome. Product (quality) risks hit the product: wrong calculations in reports, slow responses, confusing screens. Project (planning) risks hit delivery: environment and tool readiness, staff availability and skills, missing standards.

Risk can be assessed quantitatively (likelihood × impact) when you have numbers, but usually only qualitatively: very high to very low. Once a risk is identified there are four options:

  1. Mitigate it with preventive measures that reduce likelihood or impact.
  2. Make contingency plans that reduce the impact if it happens, each with a trigger and an owner.
  3. Transfer it to another party.
  4. Accept it.

Feature flags, canaries and rollback are contingency plans with automatic triggers. That's why they change how much pre-release mitigation you need.

In a mature organization, risk management happens throughout the lifecycle and at every level, not just in testing. Important risks are addressed earlier and at lower test levels: if performance is a key risk, performance tests run during unit and integration testing, not only in system test. Mature teams identify the sources of risk, use root cause analysis on the defects that do happen, and improve the process so they don't happen again. Testing takes part in, and influences, the program-wide risk analysis.

Assumptions are things you presume will be in place but can't guarantee. Reassess them regularly. Dependencies are things that must start or finish for you to progress, internal or external. Ask who you depend on and who depends on you, and manage both.

Technical debt

Technical debt is not a defect, and it doesn't go hand in hand with quality. It comes from business pressure, parallel development, missing skills, missing documentation, missing ownership, and decisions that made sense once but now slow you down. Record it, triage it, and promote it into the backlog like any other work.

  • Implementation: duplication, static rule violations, code smells.
  • Design and architecture: design smells and rule violations.
  • Test: missing tests, low coverage, fragile automation, poor test design, no non-functional tests.
  • Infrastructure: tangled dependencies, long builds, manual steps.
  • Documentation: missing, poor or outdated docs.
Test data

The integrity of test data directly affects test results; compromise it and you get false results. A test data set should be realistic, valid and versatile, and the test strategy should cover how to provision, secure, reuse and maintain it.

Understand each data element along six dimensions: type (input or existing), value (specific or a class of values), time (anything that expires, like credentials, is often why stable tests suddenly fail), reuse (an account can be reused; an order you ship can't), volume (for load and performance), and source (which database, environment or feed).

Production data has all the naturally occurring complexity that synthetic data falls short of, but copying it is tedious, it drifts when it depends on many tables, it needs obfuscation for privacy and compliance, and it can't test new functionality. Limit it to a stable subset of tests. AI-synthesized data closes much of the realism gap without the privacy risk.

Automate data creation, prerequisites and cleanup to keep pace with CI/CD, and audit the data regularly so it doesn't go stale and produce false failures.

Accessibility

Accessibility (a11y) means making a product usable by everyone, with or without disabilities. About one in seven people has some form of disability, permanent or temporary. It's the right thing to do, it's better UX for everyone, it widens your audience, and in many countries it's the law.

Myths to bust:

  • "We don't have time." You don't if it's left to the end. Make it accessibility-first.
  • "It's the testers' job." It's everyone's: developers, designers, product, test.
  • "Automated checks cover it." Tools catch roughly 20–40% of issues. Passing scans don't mean accessible.
  • "It's hard." Start with a keyboard and no mouse, then a screen reader (VoiceOver is built into the Mac; NVDA on Windows), then axe, WAVE or Lighthouse.
  • "We're WCAG AA, so we're accessible." WCAG is a great guideline. Use it with real user research, and invite real users to test. There's no such thing as 100% accessible.
Code quality and working agreements

Good code does what it should, follows a consistent style, is easy to understand, is documented, and can be tested. Keep methods small and reusable, limit arguments, handle exceptions and defaults, keep PRs small, update docs and changelogs, and cover existing behavior with tests before refactoring. Much of this belongs in static checks. Measure it with defect metrics and complexity (cyclomatic complexity is decisions + 1; high is hard to test and error-prone).

Teams should agree how they work on quality: TDD, BDD or both; pairing; who writes and runs which tests; what's in the DoD; which tools, metrics and standards. These ground rules evolve, usually out of retrospectives, and should be refactored once in a while.

    The foundations

    None of the speed above replaces the fundamentals. These are the standards and models underneath how I think about quality.

    ISO/IEC 25010: eight product quality characteristics

    The SQuaRE quality model. It superseded ISO 9126 and added security and compatibility. It gives consistent terms for specifying, measuring and evaluating quality, and a checklist to compare requirements against for completeness. There is also a quality-in-use model: effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction, freedom from risk, and context coverage.

    Functional suitabilitycompleteness, correctness, appropriateness
    Reliabilitymaturity, availability, fault tolerance, recoverability
    Performance efficiencytime behavior, resources, capacity
    Usabilitylearnability, operability, error protection, accessibility
    Securityconfidentiality, integrity, non-repudiation, accountability, authenticity
    Compatibilityco-existence, interoperability
    Maintainabilitymodularity, reusability, analysability, modifiability, testability
    Portabilityadaptability, installability, replaceability

    ISO/IEC 5055: measuring the internal structure

    ISO 5055:2021 measures source code on four business-critical factors: security, reliability, performance efficiency and maintainability. It counts weaknesses at the unit, technology and system level: hard-coded credentials, SQL injection, unhandled exceptions, unbounded resources, expensive work in loops, high cyclomatic complexity, tightly coupled modules. These are the factors that decide how trustworthy and resilient a system is, and many of them are exactly what static analysis and AI code review should catch.

    ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 and ISO 9000

    29119 is the software testing standard: concepts, processes, documentation and techniques. ISO 9000:2015 gives seven quality management principles: customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, process approach, improvement, evidence-based decision making, and relationship management. I didn't recommend aligning a delivery organization to an ISO standard; the overhead would need dedicated QA staff to be practical. They're the vocabulary and the checklist, not the process.

    Quality assurance vs. quality control

    Quality assurance is preventive and works on the process: process definition, training, audits, tool selection. Quality control is corrective and works on the product: reviews, testing, inspections. Testing sits in QC; a quality culture needs both.

    The planning hierarchy

    DocumentLevelPurposeReviewed
    Test policyCompanyWhat testing means here: objectives, standards, terms, tools, success measures, how to improveVery rarely
    Quality management planProjectQuality objectives, deliverables, standards, roles, toolsRarely
    Test strategyProductApproach aligned to business goals: scope, budget, time, standards. A living document.Rarely
    Test planRelease / sprintItems, approach, pass/fail criteria, schedule, risks, responsibilitiesRegularly
    Test caseFeatureConditions, steps, data, expected and actual resultConstantly

    Same rule as the traceability matrix: these are levels of thinking, not a stack of documents to produce. On a fast-moving team the policy is a page, the strategy lives in the repo next to the code, the plan is the story and its acceptance criteria, and the test cases are the tests. Write only what someone will read and act on.

    Testing maturity (TMMi)

    LEVEL 1InitialNo formal, documented testing. Testing is ad hoc after coding, and testing and debugging are the same thing.
    LEVEL 2ManagedTesting is separate from debugging. Policies and goals are set; basic techniques are used.
    LEVEL 3DefinedTesting is integrated into development and documented in standards, procedures and materials.
    LEVEL 4MeasuredTesting is measured and managed at the organizational level.
    LEVEL 5OptimizedData from testing is used to prevent defects and improve the process.

    Modern Testing Principles

    Alan Page's seven principles are the foundation of the whole-team quality approach I've worked toward:

    1. Our priority is improving the business.
    2. We accelerate the team, and use models like Lean Thinking and the Theory of Constraints to identify, prioritize and mitigate bottlenecks.
    3. We are a force for continuous improvement, helping the team adapt and optimize to succeed, rather than providing a safety net to catch failures.
    4. We care deeply about the quality culture of our team, and we coach, lead and nurture it toward maturity.
    5. We believe the customer is the only one capable of judging and evaluating the quality of our product.
    6. We use data extensively to understand customer usage, and close the gaps between product hypotheses and business impact.
    7. We expand testing abilities and knowhow across the team, understanding this may reduce or eliminate the need for a dedicated testing specialist.

    Speed should never be the end goal. Speed is a long-term side effect of producing code with the highest possible internal quality.
